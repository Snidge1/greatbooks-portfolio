⭐ My Most Treasured Work

Homer's Odyssey

Homer · c. 8th Century BCE · Ancient Greek Epic Poetry

Overview of the Work

The Odyssey is Homer's ancient Greek epic poem that follows the hero Odysseus (Ulysses) on his perilous ten-year journey home to Ithaca after the fall of Troy. Spanning twenty-four books of dactylic hexameter verse, it chronicles his encounters with monsters, gods, nymphs, and temptations — while his faithful wife Penelope waits and his son Telemachus comes of age in his absence. It is universally regarded as one of the foundational works of Western literature.

Why I Chose This Work

Among all the great literary masterpieces I encountered in this course, the Odyssey spoke most deeply to me because it is, at its heart, a story about longing — the primal human desire to return to the place and people where you belong. Unlike the battlefield glory of the Iliad, the Odyssey is a story of perseverance through the unglamorous, exhausting, and often invisible struggle to simply get home. That resonated with me on a deeply personal level.

Odysseus is not invincible — he weeps on Calypso's shore, he is tempted by immortality, he makes catastrophic mistakes. His humanity in the face of divine-scale obstacles makes him the most relatable of all ancient heroes. I find myself in his determination to never give up, no matter how many years the journey takes.

Personal Connection

Reading the Odyssey made me realize that every person is on their own odyssey — navigating challenges, resisting temptations, and striving toward something or someone that gives meaning to the journey. The lotus-eaters who offer forgetfulness, the Sirens who offer pleasure — these are not merely mythological creatures, but vivid metaphors for the distractions that derail us from our purpose. Homer wrote this 2,800 years ago, yet I found myself nodding at every temptation Odysseus faced, recognizing them in my own life.

Section IV

Summary of Learning

A comprehensive overview of the knowledge, insights, and skills cultivated throughout the Great Books course.

🌍

Understanding World Literature Across Civilizations

The Great Books course expanded my literary horizon far beyond the familiar. I learned to appreciate literary masterpieces from vastly different civilizations — from the Sanskrit epics of ancient India (Ramayana, Mahabharata, Panchatantra), to the Greek epic tradition (Iliad, Odyssey), to the wisdom literature of the ancient Near East (Job 38). Each civilization produced literature that was deeply rooted in its own cosmology, values, and social structure — yet each spoke to universal human truths that transcend time and geography. I discovered that despite the vast cultural distances between ancient Greece and ancient India, both celebrated heroism, wrestled with mortality, and searched for justice and meaning in a seemingly indifferent cosmos.

🔍

Mastery of Literary Lenses

Perhaps the most transformative skill I developed in this course was the ability to read the same text through multiple analytical perspectives simultaneously. The Moral/Ethical Lens taught me to evaluate the value systems embedded in narratives — asking not just what happens, but what it teaches about how to live. The Historical/Cultural Lens grounded texts in their time and place, revealing how literature both reflects and shapes society. The Archetypal/Psychological Lens showed me the deep unconscious patterns — the Hero's Journey, the Shadow, the Anima — that recur across all human storytelling. The Theological Lens opened my eyes to how ancient peoples grappled with the divine. The Socio-Political Lens revealed the power dynamics, class structures, and political wisdom encoded even in simple animal fables. Learning to wield these lenses has permanently changed how I read any text.

🪞

Literature as a Mirror of Human Experience

One of the most profound realizations of this course is that great literature endures precisely because it truthfully mirrors the human condition. The rage of Achilles in the Iliad is not an ancient Greek's rage alone — it is the rage of every person who has felt dishonored and powerless. Job's anguished questioning of divine justice is not merely a biblical narrative — it is the cry of every person who has suffered without understanding why. Draupadi's humiliation in the Mahabharata is not simply an ancient Indian woman's ordeal — it is the experience of every marginalized voice in every era demanding justice. Great Books showed me that the emotions, fears, desires, and moral struggles of people who lived millennia ago are indistinguishable from our own. Literature, I now understand, is the most powerful technology humanity has ever invented for transmitting empathy across time.

🧩

Development of Critical Thinking & Analytical Skills

Great Books fundamentally upgraded my capacity for critical reading and analytical thinking. I learned to move beyond plot summary toward genuine literary interpretation — to ask why an author made specific choices, what those choices reveal about the culture and era, and what deeper meanings are encoded beneath the surface narrative. I developed the ability to construct coherent, evidence-based literary arguments. I learned to identify motifs, symbols, and archetypes, and to trace their significance throughout a text. Most importantly, I learned that there is rarely a single "correct" interpretation of a great work — the richness of great literature lies precisely in its capacity to sustain multiple, sometimes contradictory readings. This epistemological humility — the recognition that meaning is complex and multidimensional — is perhaps the most valuable intellectual skill this course has given me.

Section V

Personal Reflection

A meaningful reflection on my overall learning journey through the Great Books course.

Q1

How has your understanding and appreciation of literature changed?

Honestly, before I took this course, I thought literature was just something you had to read for school — old stories that didn't really apply to my life as an IT student. I remember thinking, "Why do I need to read ancient epics?" But somewhere between the Ramayana and the Odyssey, that mindset completely changed. I started seeing these texts not as dusty relics but as real people writing about real struggles — just in a very different time and place.

What surprised me the most was how much I could actually relate to the characters. Odysseus just wanting to get home after everything he's been through, Rama sacrificing his throne because he refused to let his father break a promise — these things hit differently when you stop treating them as "literature" and start reading them as stories about people. My appreciation grew because I stopped trying to analyze them from a distance and just let myself get pulled in.

Q2

What challenges did you encounter, and how did you overcome them?

The biggest challenge for me was honestly just getting through the texts in the first place. Some of these works — especially the Mahabharata and the Iliad — are incredibly long and dense, and there's a lot of cultural context you need to understand before things start making sense. I remember reading Job 38 for the first time and being completely lost. God answers Job's suffering with... more questions about the universe? I had to read it multiple times before I understood what was actually happening there.

What helped me most was not trying to understand everything on the first read. I stopped pressuring myself to "get it" immediately and started treating each re-read as a chance to discover something new. I also paid more attention in class discussions — sometimes hearing a classmate's or professor's interpretation would suddenly make a whole passage click. Over time, I realized the confusion itself was part of the experience. These texts are supposed to be hard. They're asking questions that don't have easy answers.

Q3

Which literary lens influenced your interpretation the most? Why?

For me, it's the Archetypal Lens — specifically the idea of the Hero's Journey. I think what made it click for me was realizing that every single story we read followed basically the same structure: someone gets called into something bigger than themselves, faces a series of trials that changes them, and then comes back transformed. Odysseus does it. Rama does it. Even Arjuna in the Mahabharata goes through it on a battlefield.

What hit me personally was the realization that this pattern isn't just in old books — it's in my own life too. As a 4th year BSIT student, I've had my own version of that journey: the struggles of college, the moments where I wanted to quit, the small victories. Reading these epics through the archetypal lens made me feel like my own experiences were part of something bigger and older than I realized. It sounds dramatic, but it genuinely changed how I see my own story.

Q4

What important realizations about life, society, and human nature have you gained?

The biggest thing I took away from this course is something that sounds simple but actually took months to sink in: people haven't really changed. The characters in these ancient texts worry about the same things we worry about — being respected, losing people they love, doing the right thing when it's hard, wondering if their suffering means anything. Achilles throwing a fit because he felt disrespected? That happens in group chats today. The Panchatantra animals scheming and backstabbing each other for power? That's literally office politics.

It also made me realize how differently people from other cultures see the world — and how that's actually a good thing. The Greeks valued being excellent and remembered for your deeds. Ancient Indians valued duty and cosmic order. The biblical tradition valued faithfulness and humility before God. None of them are wrong, they're just asking the same questions from different angles. That taught me to be less quick to judge things I don't immediately understand, which is something I genuinely needed.

Q5

How can you apply the lessons from Great Books in your personal and professional life?

As someone going into IT, I didn't expect an English literature course to give me anything practically useful. But looking back, I think Great Books taught me more about working with people than most of my technical courses did. The Panchatantra alone is basically a guide to navigating complicated social and professional dynamics — know who to trust, don't act on impulse, and always think about the consequences before you make a move. That kind of wisdom actually applies in a workplace setting, maybe more than I'd like to admit.

Personally, the lesson I keep coming back to is from the Odyssey. Odysseus never stops trying to get home, no matter how many times things fall apart around him. I want to carry that same attitude — staying focused on where I'm going even when things get frustrating or discouraging. And from Job, I learned that it's okay to not have all the answers. Sometimes the best thing you can do is keep going and trust that clarity will come eventually. That's been genuinely helpful in my final year of college, where everything feels uncertain.
